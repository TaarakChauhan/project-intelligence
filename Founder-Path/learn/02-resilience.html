Lesson 02

Staying upright

~14 min read · Resilience

In this lesson 1. Criticism is part of the weather 2. Define your own scoreboard 3. Recovery speed matters more than thick skin myths 4. Oddness as an asset Practice checklist

Once you commit, the next pressure is social. Customers complain. Peers dismiss the idea. Family asks when you will get a “real” job. Online strangers invent motives you do not have. Resilience is not pretending this does not sting. Resilience is staying able to work while you sort signal from noise.

1. Criticism is part of the weather

Treat criticism as an environmental condition of visible work. If nobody can criticize you, you are probably not reaching enough real buyers. The skill is classification. Sort each piece of feedback into one of four bins: useful correction, preference mismatch, status attack, or incomplete information.

  • Useful correction: specific, about the work, and actionable. Thank it. Fix it. Document the change.
  • Preference mismatch: someone wants a different product. Note it. Do not redesign your whole company for one loud preference unless the market agrees.
  • Status attack: aimed at your identity, not your output. Do not debate it in public. Protect your attention.
  • Incomplete information: the critic lacks context. Clarify once if the relationship matters. Otherwise move on.

Founders get hurt most when they treat every comment as a referendum on worth. It is usually a referendum on fit, timing, or clarity. Keep your dignity by responding to the bin, not to the tone.

You do not need a thicker skin. You need a clearer sorting system and a shorter recovery loop.

2. Define your own scoreboard

If you let critics write the scoreboard, you will chase applause and avoid necessary friction. Write a private scoreboard with five to seven measures that reflect the business you are actually building. Examples: retained customers, delivery on promises, cash runway weeks, team health, learning velocity, and one quality metric customers can feel.

Review that scoreboard weekly. When criticism arrives, ask whether it changes any measure. If not, file it and continue. If yes, adjust the plan. This habit keeps you from performing for the loudest room.

Scoreboard design rules

  1. Prefer measures you can observe without a press release.
  2. Include at least one customer outcome and one internal integrity measure.
  3. Limit vanity metrics. Follower counts and compliments are optional side notes, not the game.
  4. Share a subset with your team so the company orients to the same north.

A personal scoreboard also protects relationships. When a relative asks how it is going, you can answer with real progress instead of defensiveness. Clarity lowers the temperature.

3. Recovery speed matters more than thick skin myths

Everyone gets knocked off balance. The difference is how quickly you return to useful work. Build a recovery routine you can run in under thirty minutes after a hard hit.

  1. Name it. Write one sentence: what happened, and what you feel. Do not polish it.
  2. Sort it. Assign the criticism bin. Extract any useful correction in one bullet.
  3. Body reset. Walk, breathe, or change rooms. Physical state change shortens rumination.
  4. Next action. Choose one task that advances a customer, a delivery, or a hire. Start it for fifteen minutes.
  5. Optional debrief. Later that day, ask a trusted peer for a five-minute reality check if needed.

Track recovery time for a month. If a comment still owns your afternoon three days later, the issue is not the comment. It is an unmet need for clarity, support, or rest. Fix the system, not just the mood.

Also separate privacy from secrecy. You can share struggle with a small circle without performing struggle for an audience. Public venting often prolongs the event and invites more status attacks.

4. Oddness as an asset

Many durable companies look slightly strange at the start. The packaging is unusual. The sales motion is more personal than competitors expect. The founder cares about a detail most people skip. That oddness is often the edge. Do not sand it off to look “professional” if it is how customers recognize you.

Protect productive oddness. Drop unproductive oddness. Productive oddness creates value customers will pay for: a weirdly careful onboarding, a pricing model that aligns incentives, a support style that feels human. Unproductive oddness creates friction without value: unclear naming, ego rituals, or process theater.

Odd traitKeep if…Drop if…
Very long demosBuyers convert and thank you for depthBuyers stall and ask for a shorter path
Handmade follow-up notesRelationships deepen and referrals riseIt delays delivery and does not move deals
Strict quality gatesDefects fall and trust risesGates become delay without quality gains

Resilience includes the courage to remain distinctive while still listening. You are not fragile because you care. You are fragile when you outsource your standards to people who do not carry the consequences.

5. Public rooms and private rooms

Not every criticism deserves a public reply. Public rooms reward heat. Private rooms reward clarity. When a customer is hurt, respond in the channel that restores trust fastest, usually direct and specific. When a stranger is performing contempt, starve the performance of attention.

Create a personal policy in advance: which platforms you will answer on, response time targets for customers versus spectators, and who on your team may speak for the company. Policies reduce panic decisions made at midnight.

Also design your private room: a journal, a coach, a peer founder, or a partner who can hear the unedited version. Resilience without a private room turns into repression, and repressed frustration leaks into hiring and customer tone.

6. Body, sleep, and the story you tell

Emotional recovery is partly physiology. Sleep debt, skipped meals, and permanent caffeine spikes make ordinary criticism feel existential. Protect basic inputs the way you protect server uptime. This is not wellness branding. It is operational hygiene for judgment.

Watch the story you tell after a hit. “They are all against me” and “I am finished” are dramatic and usually false. Replace them with “This was a preference mismatch” or “This was a useful correction I will ship by Friday.” Language shapes recovery speed.

Practice one weekly review where you list criticisms received, bins assigned, actions taken, and emotional recovery time. Patterns will appear. Maybe one customer segment triggers you because the offer is unclear. Maybe one relative’s comments linger because you still want approval you no longer need for the work. Patterns make the next month quieter.

7. Allies, not audiences

Resilient founders collect allies: customers who want you to win, peers who trade hard truths, and teammates who share the scoreboard. Audiences collect reactions. Build ally loops with reciprocity. Share useful notes. Ask for specific feedback. Offer help without keeping score in public.

When criticism arrives, ask an ally for a second read before you rewrite the company. One trusted counterweight prevents both denial and overcorrection. Overcorrection is a quiet form of fragility. It looks responsive while it abandons strategy.

Practice checklist

  • Create a four-bin criticism sorting note and use it on the next three pieces of feedback.
  • Write a private weekly scoreboard with five to seven measures.
  • Design a thirty-minute recovery routine and time it after the next hard comment.
  • List two odd traits you will protect and one you will retire.
  • Tell one trusted person your scoreboard so you have a reality partner.
