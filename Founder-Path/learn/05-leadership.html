Lesson 05

Leading from the front

~15 min read · Leadership

In this lesson 1. Ownership that is visible 2. Setting pace without chaos 3. Making unpopular calls 4. Growing other leaders 5. Handing off authority Practice checklist

Leadership in a small company is less about speeches and more about the example people can copy on a Tuesday afternoon. You set the standard for ownership, pace, honesty, and how hard problems get handled. If those signals are muddy, the team invents its own, usually in conflicting directions.

1. Ownership that is visible

Ownership means you pick up problems that sit between roles, you admit mistakes early, and you do not outsource blame to “the market” when the miss was local. Visible ownership looks like naming the issue, naming the next step, and naming the owner of that step, even when the owner is you.

  • Start status updates with reality, not spin.
  • When you were wrong, say so before someone else has to.
  • Finish loops. Open loops teach the team that follow-through is optional.

People will forgive uneven strategy longer than they forgive slippery accountability. Be the person who makes accountability normal and non-humiliating.

2. Setting pace without chaos

Pace is the rhythm of decisions and delivery. Too slow and opportunities expire. Too frantic and quality collapses. Leaders set pace by what they reward, what they interrupt, and how they schedule.

Choose a default cadence: daily for critical delivery windows, weekly for priorities, monthly for strategy. Protect deep work blocks. If every day is an emergency meeting, you are not setting pace. You are leaking anxiety into the calendar.

Your calendar is a leadership document. It tells the team what actually matters.

3. Making unpopular calls

Some decisions will disappoint good people: killing a pet feature, changing pricing, exiting a client, delaying a hire, or ending a project that consumed pride. Unpopular does not mean cruel. It means you choose the long-term health of the mission over short-term comfort.

  1. State the decision in plain language.
  2. Explain the criteria you used.
  3. Acknowledge the cost to people who invested effort.
  4. Describe the next constructive move.
  5. Invite questions, not endless re-litigation.

Delaying an unpopular call often creates a more unpopular outcome later. Practice smaller hard calls so the larger ones do not feel like identity threats.

4. Growing other leaders

If every hard problem returns to you, you have a bottleneck with a title. Growing others means assigning outcomes (not just tasks), coaching through first attempts, and celebrating independent judgment that aligns with standards.

Use after-action reviews that ask what happened, what we learned, and what we will change. Keep them short and free of blame theater. People grow faster when learning is routine.

5. Handing off authority

Authority handoff is a designed transfer, not a shrug. Define the domain, the budget or risk limits, the escalation rules, and the review cadence. Then stop re-deciding inside that domain unless escalation criteria are met.

If you hand off and then constantly override, you taught distrust. If you hand off with no boundaries, you taught gambling. Clear rails create speed.

Start with one domain this month. Write the handoff page. Review it after four weeks. Adjust rails, not the person’s dignity.

6. Communication that reduces rumor

In small teams, silence gets filled with stories. Lead with a steady communication rhythm: weekly priorities, decision logs for major calls, and open time for questions. You do not need a corporate intranet. You need fewer surprises.

When information is sensitive, say what you can say and name what you cannot share yet. People handle constraints better than fog. Fog breeds politics.

7. Conflict as maintenance

Conflict avoided becomes culture rot. Teach a simple conflict method: state the observed behavior, state the impact, state the request, and invite the other person’s view. Use it for peer conflicts and for founder-to-team conflicts. Model it when you are the one who caused impact.

Separate relationship repair from decision disagreement. You can disagree on a tactic and still keep respect. Leaders who personalize every disagreement train people to hide information.

8. Energy leadership

Your mood is contagious, especially in a company of ten or fewer. This does not mean fake cheer. It means you regulate enough to be usable. If you are depleted, say you are taking a recovery block rather than spraying irritability across standups.

Celebrate the behaviors you want repeated: clean handoffs, early risk flags, customer care, and calm under pressure. What you celebrate becomes the real performance review, regardless of what the handbook says.

9. Presence in the work

Leading from the front includes periodic presence in the actual work: a support shift, a sales call, a delivery review. Presence keeps your standards grounded in reality. It also signals that no work is beneath dignity. Then leave enough space for owners to own. Presence is not permanent takeover.

11. The example inventory

Once a month, inventory the examples you set: how you handled a miss, how you treated a junior teammate, how you spoke about a customer who frustrated you, how you managed your own deadlines. Teams imitate what you do under mild stress more than what you announce in all-hands meetings.

Where the example is weak, pick one behavior to correct in public through action, not announcement. If you were late with feedback, become early for two weeks. If you interrupted, practice letting others finish. Leadership development is often this unglamorous.

Ask a trusted teammate for one example you are setting that helps and one that hurts. Listen without defense. Thank them. Change one thing. That loop builds credibility faster than charisma.

12. Worked example: the unpopular product cut

Your team loves a feature that few customers use. Keeping it dilutes support and slows the roadmap. You call a meeting, show usage and support cost, acknowledge the craft invested, announce the sunset date, and assign owners for migration notes. Some people are disappointed. Customers who never used it barely notice. Capacity returns to the work that pays for payroll.

If you avoid the cut, disappointment still arrives later as missed deadlines and quiet cynicism. Leadership is often the willingness to take a smaller pain now to prevent a larger pain later, and to explain that trade in adult language.

13. The trust bank

Every kept promise deposits trust. Every surprise withdrawal spends it. Track your personal trust bank with the team: commitments made in meetings, deadlines you set for yourself, and feedback you promised. If your balance is low, pause new vision speeches and rebuild through small kept promises for two weeks.

Trust banks also exist with customers and partners. Leadership from the front means you know which banks are low and you prioritize deposits there before launching new campaigns.

Practice checklist

  • In your next team update, lead with one uncomfortable truth and one next step.
  • Audit your calendar for pace: cut one recurring meeting that creates noise.
  • Make one overdue unpopular call using the five-step script.
  • Assign an outcome (not a task list) to someone and schedule a coaching check-in.
  • Write a one-page authority handoff for a single domain.
