Lesson 04

Seats that matter

~15 min read · Hiring

In this lesson 1. The real cost of a wrong hire 2. A-player standards without mystique 3. Culture as daily behavior 4. Recruiting as a founder skill 5. What strong people actually want Practice checklist

Hiring is leverage and risk in the same act. A strong hire multiplies quality and pace. A weak hire multiplies confusion, rework, and quiet resentment. Treat every seat as a design decision, not a staffing reflex.

1. The real cost of a wrong hire

Salary is the visible cost. The hidden costs are larger: manager time spent correcting, team morale dips, customer trust leaks, and delayed work that never shows on a resume. Add the cost of exiting poorly and recruiting again. When you price a hire correctly, patience becomes rational.

  • Estimate salary plus tools plus onboarding time for ninety days.
  • Estimate hours you will spend coaching if the person is a stretch.
  • Estimate downside if the person fails with customers.
  • Compare that total to waiting four more weeks for a stronger candidate.

Urgency is often a process failure, not a market failure. If you are always hiring in panic, fix forecasting and cross-training before you lower the bar.

2. A-player standards without mystique

An A-player, in practical terms, is someone who reliably produces excellent work in the seat, raises the people around them, and needs less supervision than the average hire. The label is useless if you cannot define the seat outcomes.

Write a one-page seat scorecard before you post a role: mission of the seat, outcomes in six months, competencies that predict those outcomes, and examples of work product you want to see in interviews. Interview against that page. Do not invent criteria mid-conversation because someone was charming.

Signals that hold up

  1. Specific stories of ownership with measurable results.
  2. Evidence they sought feedback and changed behavior.
  3. References that match the stories without heavy coaching from the candidate.
  4. Work samples that look like your real environment, not a polished portfolio only.

Avoid false proxies. Pedigree, buzzwords, and confidence in interviews can hide weak execution. Prefer proof.

3. Culture as daily behavior

Culture is what people do when the founder is not in the room. Write the behaviors you reward: candor with care, finishing work, keeping promises to customers, helping teammates without theater. Then hire for those behaviors. A brilliant individual who poisons trust is an expensive liability.

During interviews, ask for examples of conflict, missed deadlines, and ethical gray areas. Listen for blame patterns. People who never made a mistake either have not done hard work or cannot tell the truth yet.

Culture statements on a wall mean little. Hiring and exit decisions teach the real curriculum.

4. Recruiting as a founder skill

Waiting for inbound applications is not a strategy. Recruiting means building a living list of people you admire, keeping light contact, writing clear outreach, and moving quickly when mutual interest appears. Block time each week for sourcing, just as you would for sales.

  • Keep a warm list of twenty to fifty names by role.
  • Send short, specific notes that reference real work you have seen.
  • Share the problem and the scorecard early. Strong people want clarity.
  • Decide fast after due diligence. Slow ghosting loses A-players to sharper teams.

Also train interviewers. Unstructured chats produce inconsistent yes votes. Use the same core questions and a written rubric. Debrief with evidence, not vibes.

5. What strong people actually want

Top people want meaningful problems, competent colleagues, clear expectations, room to decide, fair pay, and a leader who tells the truth about risk. Fancy perks are secondary. If your pitch is mostly lifestyle branding, you will attract people optimizing for lifestyle.

GrowthHard problems and coachingMicromanagement theater
TrustHonest runway and prioritiesSurprise crises you hid
RespectOn-time pay and credit sharedPublic blame

Sell the seat the way you sell to customers: truthfully, specifically, and with a path to success. Then protect the people who join by keeping the operating system clean.

6. Onboarding as part of hiring

The hire is not complete when the offer is signed. The first thirty days teach the new person what standards are real. Prepare a thirty-day plan before day one: tools access, first wins, meeting map, and a named buddy for questions. Ambiguous onboarding creates early underperformance that looks like a bad hire.

Set a two-way check-in schedule. Ask what is confusing. Ask what is surprisingly good. Give feedback early while change is cheap. Waiting sixty days to share concerns is a leadership failure dressed as patience.

7. Exit with dignity and speed

When the seat is wrong, delay helps nobody. Document performance gaps against the scorecard. Offer a short, fair chance to correct if the gaps are coachable. If they are not, exit cleanly: clear reason, fair logistics, and protected customer handoffs.

Gossip after an exit teaches fear. Facts and care teach standards. Do not trash people. Do not pretend the miss did not happen. Teams watch exits to learn whether scorecards were real.

8. Compensation clarity

Pay in a way you can explain. Confusion about bonus rules, equity myths, or variable pay creates silent distrust. Write the rules. Share ranges when you can. If you cannot pay market peak yet, be honest about stage, upside, and constraints. Strong candidates can accept tradeoffs they understand. They resent tradeoffs sprung as surprises.

Revisit compensation as the company changes. A package that was fair at five people can become unfair at fifteen without anyone intending harm. Schedule compensation reviews the way you schedule financial reviews.

9. Hiring for the team you are becoming

Do not hire only for today’s fire. Hire for the system you are building in the next two seasons, while still ensuring the person can produce now. Pure future bets with no near-term seat outcomes are expensive speculation. Pure firefighting hires with no growth trajectory create churn.

Write both horizons on the scorecard: outcomes for the first six months, and the larger role the seat may grow into if the person thrives. Clarity attracts people who want ownership, not just a temporary badge.

11. Broad search, consistent standards

Widen where you look so you do not recycle the same narrow network. Consistent standards across candidates protect fairness and quality. Changing the bar mid-search because you are tired is how wrong hires sneak in. If you are tired, pause the search rather than lower the seat.

Train interviewers on bias toward familiarity, confidence theater, and similarity. Ask for evidence against the scorecard. Diversity of background helps only if evaluation stays disciplined.

12. Worked example: the rushed sales hire

Imagine pipeline is soft and you want a salesperson yesterday. Without a scorecard, you hire a confident talker who cannot run discovery or write clear proposals. Ninety days later, you have burned leads, confused pricing, and a team that distrusts sales. The tuition is not only salary. It is damaged market trust.

The alternative is slower and kinder to the company: write the seat outcomes, source twenty names, run structured interviews, check references against stories, and keep founder-led selling until the seat is truly filled. Patience here is a growth strategy, not a delay tactic.

Practice checklist

  • Write a one-page scorecard for your next open seat before sourcing.
  • Estimate the full cost of a wrong hire for that seat.
  • List ten culture behaviors you will hire and fire for.
  • Build or refresh a warm candidate list of at least twenty names.
  • Draft an honest role pitch covering problem, expectations, and risks.
