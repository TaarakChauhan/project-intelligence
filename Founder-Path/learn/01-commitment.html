Lesson 01

The commitment test

~14 min read · Readiness

In this lesson 1. Check the motive 2. Passion that survives boredom 3. What you will fight for 4. Personal strengths in the open 5. Whether to start Practice checklist

Before you raise money, hire anyone, or print business cards, run a quiet commitment test. Founding is not a personality style. It is a multi-year vow to solve a specific problem for specific people while the work stays hard longer than the excitement lasts. This lesson helps you decide with clear eyes, not with social pressure or a romantic story about entrepreneurship.

The goal is not to talk yourself into starting. The goal is to learn whether you have a durable reason to continue when novelty fades, cash is tight, and the calendar fills with unglamorous tasks.

1. Check the motive

Write down, in one paragraph, why you want this venture. Then rewrite it without status language. Remove words about proving people wrong, looking successful, escaping a job you dislike, or becoming someone who “owns something.” What remains is closer to your operating motive.

Useful motives tend to sound ordinary and stubborn. You keep seeing the same broken workflow. You care about a customer segment that is ignored. You have a craft you want to practice at scale. Weak motives tend to sound like costume changes. They collapse when the costume stops getting applause.

Three motive filters

  1. Service filter. Can you name the person who benefits if you succeed, and how their day gets better?
  2. Duration filter. Would you still care about this problem if recognition took five years?
  3. Trade filter. Which comforts are you willing to trade for progress: free evenings, a clean inbox, social certainty?

If you cannot pass those filters, pause. You may still build something later. Starting now would mainly train you to quit under predictable stress.

A clean motive does not guarantee success. A muddy motive almost guarantees a fragile commitment.

2. Passion that survives boredom

Early passion is cheap. It shows up in idea sessions, branding debates, and first launches. Durable passion shows up in reconciliation work, support tickets, and the twentieth revision of a proposal. Ask yourself which parts of the work you can do on a flat Tuesday when no one is watching.

Make a two-column list. Column A: tasks that energize you. Column B: tasks that are necessary even when they drain you. Founders who last accept that Column B is not a bug. It is the job. Your commitment is credible when you can name how you will handle Column B without resentment eating the business.

  • Will you schedule boring work first, while energy is high?
  • Will you hire for gaps only after you have done the work enough to teach it?
  • Will you measure progress in shipped outcomes, not in how inspired you feel?

Passion that survives boredom looks like curiosity about the craft, not adrenaline about the identity. If you only love the announcement, you do not love the company yet.

3. What you will fight for

Commitment becomes real when something is worth defending. That might be product quality, customer trust, a pricing stance, or a culture rule. Without a short list of non-negotiables, every conflict becomes a negotiation with yourself, and you will bargain away the center of the work.

Write three sentences that begin with “I will fight for…” Keep them concrete. “I will fight for clear contracts” is better than “I will fight for excellence.” “I will fight for weekly customer contact” is better than “I will fight for innovation.”

Examples of usable non-negotiables

  • We do not ship work we would be ashamed to explain to a careful customer.
  • We answer serious support issues the same day they arrive.
  • We do not hire people we would not trust unsupervised with a client.
  • We keep one weekly metric review even when the week feels chaotic.

Then write the opposite: what you will not fight for. Many founders waste energy defending preferences that do not matter. Letting go of vanity battles frees capacity for the few hills that actually define the company.

4. Personal strengths in the open

Commitment is stronger when it is built on strengths you can demonstrate, not on strengths you wish you had. Inventory what you already do well under load: selling, writing, systems, recruiting, technical build, negotiation, teaching. Ask two people who have worked with you for their blunt view. Compare notes.

Then map strengths to the next twelve months of the venture. If your strength is customer conversations and the next year is mostly product architecture you do not enjoy and cannot staff, your commitment will be strained by design. Either reshape the plan, find a complementary partner, or delay until the match is honest.

StrengthNear-term useRisk if ignored
Clear writingOffers, proposals, onboarding docsMessy promises and support debt
Calm sales talksPipeline and discovery callsWaiting for “marketing” to save you
Operational detailDelivery checklists and quality gatesHeroic delivery that does not scale
Coaching peopleEarly hiring and feedback loopsLone-wolf bottleneck

Strengths are not a permission slip to avoid growth. They are a starting point for a plan you can keep. Commitment without a realistic strength map is mostly hope with a logo.

5. Whether to start

Use a yes, not yet, or no decision. Avoid “maybe forever.”

Yes when motive is clean, you can tolerate the boring core of the work, you have non-negotiables, and your strengths cover the first critical path (or you have a real partner who covers the gap).

Not yet when the idea is promising but you still need skill, savings runway, customer evidence, or a partner. Write the conditions that would flip not yet into yes. Date them. Revisit on that date.

No when the motive is mostly escape or image, you hate the actual work, or you cannot name who benefits. A clean no is a form of integrity. It protects your reputation and frees you for work you can commit to.

If you decide yes, make the commitment visible in calendar form. Block weekly time for customer contact, product or service improvement, and financial review. Commitment that never appears on a calendar is still a wish.

6. Personal runway and life design

Commitment is not only psychological. It is logistical. Map your personal runway in weeks: savings, expected revenue timing, and fixed costs. Then map life design constraints: caregiving, health, and relationships that need protected time. A plan that ignores these will feel like a moral failure when it is actually a planning failure.

Talk with anyone who shares your household economics. Hidden resentment is a silent co-founder conflict. Agree on review dates for money and energy. Agree on what “pause” would look like if runway thins, so fear does not invent a chaotic exit later.

Also decide what you will stop doing for the first season. Every yes to founding is a no to something else. Write the nos. Unwritten nos return as guilt and half-finished side quests.

A simple readiness score

Score yourself from 1 to 5 on motive clarity, boredom tolerance, non-negotiables, strength-fit, runway weeks, and household alignment. Do not average your way into a fantasy. If two or more scores are at 2 or below, choose not yet and build those scores on purpose.

Readiness is dynamic. Revisit the score monthly during the first year. Commitment that cannot be re-examined becomes dogma. Commitment that is re-examined without collapsing at every bump becomes maturity.

7. Solo versus partnered commitment

If you plan to partner, run the same commitment test independently, then compare answers. Misaligned motives are harder to repair than misaligned skills. Discuss decision rights, equity expectations, working hours, and exit clauses while everyone is still polite. Polite silence is expensive later.

If you plan to go solo, name the advisory circle that will challenge you. Solo founders do not need a chorus. They need two or three people who will tell them when the motive has drifted into status theater or avoidance.

Either path can work. Pretending you have partnership clarity when you only have enthusiasm does not work. Write the operating agreement concepts even if lawyers come later: who decides what, how conflict gets handled, and what happens if someone wants out.

Practice checklist

  • Write a one-paragraph motive with status language removed.
  • List five boring tasks the business will require every month, and how you will handle each.
  • Define three non-negotiables and two preferences you will stop defending.
  • Ask two colleagues for your top two strengths under pressure; compare with your self-view.
  • Record a dated decision: yes, not yet (with conditions), or no.
