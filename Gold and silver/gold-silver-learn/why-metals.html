Why Gold and Silver

Among candidate commodities, gold and silver rose repeatedly because their properties align unusually well with money’s jobs.

  • Durability. They resist corrosion and do not spoil, unlike grain or livestock.
  • Divisibility and fusibility. Soft metals can be melted and reminted into pieces sized for large or small payments.
  • Portability. High value-to-weight ratios pack substantial purchasing power into small bulk, easing long-distance trade.
  • Scarcity. Annual new production is limited relative to existing stocks, supporting money’s store-of-value role—though large discoveries (New World silver; nineteenth-century gold rushes) can still disturb prices.
  • Uniformity and recognisability. Once refined and stamped, coins of known weight and fineness are easier to verify than heterogeneous goods.

Saleableness

Carl Menger’s classic account of money’s emergence stresses “saleableness”: traders favour goods that others will readily accept across time and place. Gold and silver scored highly on that criterion. Their monetary status was partly spontaneous market selection and partly later reinforced by state coinage, legal tender laws, and mint monopolies.

Key takeaway

Gold and silver won not by decree alone, but because they were durable, scarce, and widely “saleable”—qualities markets and states then locked in with stamps and law.
