Coins & History

Coinage cut verification costs. Regional paths then diverged—Mediterranean metals, Chinese paper then silver, Indian silver then a sterling peg.

Lydia, Greece, and Rome

According to a widely accepted historical consensus, the earliest precious-metal coinage emerged in the kingdom of Lydia (western Anatolia, now Turkey) in the second half of the seventh century BCE, using electrum—an alloy of gold and silver. Stamped pieces cut verification costs: merchants could count rather than reweigh every lump. The World Gold Council notes that gold coins were struck on the order of King Croesus of Lydia around 550 BCE.

Greek city-states soon adopted silver coinage extensively; Rome developed a layered system of silver (such as the denarius) and gold issues alongside base-metal small change. Debasement—reducing metal content while keeping face value—was already a recurring fiscal temptation in antiquity, as it would be later.

China, India, and silver

China followed a different path. Early Chinese money included bronze cowrie imitations and tool-shaped pieces before round cash coins. China was also first to use paper money extensively (from roughly the seventh century CE). The Bank of England notes that later Ming efforts to replace coins with paper contributed to over-issue and inflation. After paper failures, uncoined silver—weighed by the tael—became central to large trade and, under Ming fiscal reforms culminating in the Single Whip Reform (1581), to tax payments. China remained largely on silver into the early twentieth century while most industrial economies converged on gold (World Gold Council).

In South Asia, the silver rupiya associated with Sher Shah Suri in the mid-sixteenth century became a lasting template. British India’s Coinage Act of 1835 established a uniform monometallic silver rupee. After silver’s gold price fell from the 1870s, the rupee depreciated against sterling, raising the cost of India’s sterling obligations. India closed mints to free silver coinage in 1893 and moved toward a gold-exchange standard by the late 1890s, pegging the rupee to sterling while silver coins continued to circulate—showing how silver standards interacted with a gold-centred world.

Bimetallism

Bimetallism makes both gold and silver full legal tender at a legally fixed ratio. The U.S. Coinage Act of 1792 defined the dollar in both metals (Britannica cites 24.75 grains of gold or 371.25 grains of silver). When the mint ratio diverged from the market ratio, Gresham’s Law tended to operate: the legally overvalued metal drove the undervalued metal out of circulation. Nineteenth-century metal discoveries repeatedly unsettled bimetallic systems, pushing many countries toward gold monometallism.

Key takeaway

Coinage turned metal into trusted tokens; regional paths diverged—Mediterranean gold and silver, Chinese paper then silver, Indian silver then a sterling peg—while bimetallism often buckled when legal ratios drifted from market prices.

Commodity versus representative money

Commodity money derives value primarily from the object itself—gold coins, silver rupees, or weighed bullion. Representative money is a claim on a commodity held in reserve: a warehouse receipt, goldsmith’s note, or banknote redeemable in metal on demand.

In seventeenth-century London, goldsmiths accepted coin deposits and issued receipts. The Bank of England explains that these “running cash notes” promised to pay the bearer on demand and became convenient payment instruments; similar wording still appears on Bank of England notes. When the Bank was founded in 1694, it issued notes as deposit receipts that evolved into modern banknotes. China had pioneered paper money centuries earlier; Sweden issued European banknotes in 1661.

Representative money economised on moving heavy metal and enabled fractional reserves, expanding liquidity—but also introducing runs, over-issue, and wartime suspensions of convertibility. Britain’s Restriction Period (1797–1821) is a clear example of convertibility paused under fiscal and military strain.
