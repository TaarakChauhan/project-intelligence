How Fiat Emerged

Wars, depression, and the unraveling of Bretton Woods turned temporary suspensions into a permanent floating world.

Wartime and Depression suspensions

Fiat episodes are not uniquely modern. U.S. Civil War greenbacks (Legal Tender Act, 1862) were paper legal tender not backed by gold or silver. Wartime and crisis suspensions repeatedly showed that convertibility could be postponed when survival or macro-stabilisation seemed to require it. The twentieth century made such breaks systemic rather than temporary.

Bretton Woods

In July 1944, delegates from forty-four nations met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. As Federal Reserve History explains, they created the IMF and what became the World Bank Group. Currencies were fixed but adjustable to the U.S. dollar, and the dollar was fixed to gold at $35 an ounce. The system became fully operational for convertibility around 1958. Unlike the classical gold standard, Bretton Woods allowed capital controls and placed the dollar—not circulating gold coin—at the centre of settlement.

The arrangement contained a structural tension later labelled the Triffin dilemma: the world needed growing dollar reserves, but persistent U.S. deficits eventually made foreign-held dollars exceed U.S. gold at the official price. The London Gold Pool (1961–1968) and Federal Reserve swap lines were temporary defences. Vietnam War spending, domestic programmes, and inflation intensified the strain by the late 1960s.

The Nixon Shock and pure fiat

On 15 August 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended dollar convertibility into gold for foreign official holders—closing the “gold window”—alongside a wage-price freeze and a 10 percent import surcharge. Federal Reserve History and the U.S. State Department Office of the Historian describe this “Nixon Shock” as the beginning of the end of Bretton Woods.

The Smithsonian Agreement (December 1971) tried to restore pegs around a devalued dollar; by March 1973 major currencies floated. The IMF’s Second Amendment (Jamaica 1976; effective 1978) formally accepted floating rates, eliminated gold’s official monetary role in the Fund, and emphasised surveillance. Since then, national fiat currencies managed by central banks have predominated, with gold retained as a reserve asset rather than the legal anchor of money.

Key takeaway

Fiat did not arrive overnight; wars, depression, and the unraveling of Bretton Woods turned temporary suspensions into a permanent, dollar-centred floating world.

What fiat means today

Encyclopaedia Britannica defines fiat money as money made legal tender by government decree (“fiat”). In ordinary usage it means legal-tender paper or token coins whose face values far exceed commodity values and that are not redeemable in gold or silver.

Value rests on three pillars:

  • Legal tender status — creditors must generally accept it for debts in that unit
  • Trust in the issuing authority and the fiscal-monetary framework
  • Network effects — people accept money because others accept it for goods, taxes, and wages

Modern bank deposits and electronic payments are mostly private liabilities denominated in fiat units under public regulation.
