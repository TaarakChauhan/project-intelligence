Why Money at All

Before money, exchange often meant barter—and barter hits hard limits as societies grow.

Barter works in small, repeated communities, but it stumbles on the coincidence of wants: a fisher who wants berries must find a berry-picker who wants fish, at the same moment, in matching quantities. As the Bank of England notes in its educational history of money, seasonal goods, perishable produce, and specialised skills make such matches unreliable. Accounting, credit, and commodity intermediaries therefore appeared long before anyone minted a coin.

Early societies used many media of exchange—livestock, cowry shells, grain, copper tools, and weighed metals. The Bank of England observes that cows became units of exchange after domestication, and that cowry shells circulated widely in Asia, Africa, and Oceania (with early documented use in China around 1300 BCE).

Three functions of money

Money, in this sense, is less a pure government invention than a practical answer to the high transaction costs of direct barter and the need for three functions:

  • Unit of account — a shared yardstick for comparing value
  • Medium of exchange — a portable claim others will accept
  • Store of value — something that holds purchasing power over time
Key takeaway

Money emerges where barter’s frictions bite hardest—when people need a shared yardstick for value and a portable claim that others will accept.
