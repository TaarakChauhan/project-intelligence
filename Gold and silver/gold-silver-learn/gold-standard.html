The Gold Standard and Bimetallism

A system in which the monetary unit is a fixed quantity of gold—or freely convertible into gold at a fixed price.

Britannica defines the gold standard as a system in which the monetary unit is a fixed quantity of gold or is freely convertible into gold at a fixed price. Britain consolidated a gold standard in the early nineteenth century (Britannica cites 1821). Most other countries remained on silver or bimetallism until the 1870s.

The classical international gold standard

The classical international gold standard is generally dated from the 1870s to 1914. The World Gold Council notes that newly unified Germany’s move toward gold after the Franco-Prussian War encouraged others to follow, alongside Britain’s financial dominance and shifts in relative metal supplies. By about 1900, nearly all major economies except China and some Central American countries were on gold.

Currencies were convertible at fixed parities; gold could cross borders; exchange rates were tightly constrained. David Hume’s price-specie-flow mechanism described self-correction via gold flows and prices—though central banks used discount rates and did not always follow the “rules of the game” perfectly.

Silver politics in the United States

Silver and bimetallism remained politically charged. In the United States, the Coinage Act of 1873 ended free coinage of silver dollars (the “Crime of ’73” to silver interests). Debates culminated in William Jennings Bryan’s 1896 “Cross of Gold” speech defending free silver against deflation associated with tight gold money. The Gold Standard Act of 1900 formalised a U.S. gold standard.

War and depression snap the links

World War I shattered classical convertibility. Interwar gold-exchange restorations proved fragile; Britain left gold in 1931; the United States ended domestic convertibility in 1933 and in 1934 revalued gold to $35 per ounce—the price later used at Bretton Woods.

Key takeaway

The classical gold standard knitted currencies to metal and to each other—until war, politics, and depression snapped the links country by country.
