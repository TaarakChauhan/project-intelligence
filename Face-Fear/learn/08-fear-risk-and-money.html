Fear, risk, and money decisions

Loss aversion, panic selling, and how to make good decisions when fear is loud.

Fear is useful when you face a real risk. It becomes expensive when it makes the decision for you. Money is where this shows up most clearly, because markets, prices, and jobs give fear endless material.

Losses loom larger

In 1979, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described what they called prospect theory. One of its central findings is loss aversion. People tend to feel a loss more strongly than a gain of the same size. Losing a hundred dollars feels worse than finding a hundred dollars feels good.

Loss aversion can protect you from reckless bets. It can also keep you in a bad job, a failing project, or cash that loses value to inflation, simply because changing feels like risking a loss.

Looking too often makes fear worse

Shlomo Benartzi and Richard Thaler suggested that people who check their investments often see more small losses, and so feel more fear, even when the long term trend is up. They called this myopic loss aversion. It can push people to sell at the worst times.

A simple fix is to choose how often you look, and to set rules in advance for when you will act.

Fear versus risk

Fear is a feeling. Risk is a measure of how likely and how bad an outcome is. They often do not match. People fear flying more than driving, even though driving is far riskier per trip. People fear a market drop more than slow, steady overspending.

Good decisions ask two separate questions. What does the evidence say about the risk? And what is my fear telling me that I should listen to?

Rules for deciding while afraid

  • Name the feeling before you decide. I am scared of losing money.
  • Write the worst realistic outcome, the best, and the most likely.
  • Ask what you would advise a friend in the same position.
  • Set a waiting period for big decisions made in fear, such as 24 or 48 hours.
  • Decide your rules when you are calm, and follow them when you are not.
  • Ask whether the decision is reversible. Reversible decisions deserve less fear.

Key ideas

  • Loss aversion means losses feel bigger than equal gains.
  • Checking investments too often can increase fear and lead to poor timing.
  • Fear and actual risk often do not match. Evaluate them separately.
  • Rules made while calm protect decisions made while afraid.

Try this: The fear and risk split

  1. Pick one money or career decision you are putting off.
  2. On the left, write what your fear says will happen.
  3. On the right, write what the evidence says, including base rates if you can find them.
  4. Write the worst realistic case and how you would recover from it.
  5. Make one reversible move this week.
