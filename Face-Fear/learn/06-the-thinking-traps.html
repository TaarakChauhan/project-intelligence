The thinking traps

Catastrophising, threat appraisal, uncertainty, and the avoidance loop that keeps fear alive.

Fear is not only about what happens. It is also about how you read what happens. The same event can feel like a small problem or a disaster depending on the story you tell yourself in the first few seconds.

Appraisal: the story you tell

Psychologists use the word appraisal for the quick judgement you make about a situation. Is this a threat? How bad could it be? Can I cope? Fear tends to grow when you rate the danger as high and your ability to cope as low.

Both halves can be worked on. You can check how likely and how bad the danger really is. You can also remind yourself of times you coped with hard things before.

Common traps

  • Catastrophising: jumping to the worst possible outcome. One mistake means I will be fired.
  • Overestimating the odds: treating something possible as if it were likely.
  • Underestimating yourself: assuming you could not handle it if it happened.
  • Mind reading: assuming you know what others think, usually something negative.
  • Fortune telling: being sure about a future you cannot see.

The trouble with uncertainty

Many people can cope with bad news better than with not knowing. Researchers call the struggle with not knowing intolerance of uncertainty. It shows up across many forms of anxiety, which is why some researchers see fear of the unknown as a basic fear underneath many others.

People who struggle with uncertainty often try to create certainty by checking, researching, asking for reassurance, or over planning. These give short relief but teach the brain that uncertainty is unbearable.

The avoidance loop

Avoidance is the engine that keeps most fears running. When you avoid something, you feel instant relief. Relief feels good, so the brain learns to avoid again. And because you never face the situation, you never learn that you could have handled it.

Safety behaviours are a quieter form of avoidance. Over preparing a speech word for word, avoiding eye contact, always sitting near the door, or keeping a drink in hand. They feel helpful, but they can stop you from learning that you were safe all along. The pull toward quick relief is closely related to instant gratification, which has its own section in this project.

Stepping out

You do not need to argue with every fearful thought. Often it is enough to notice it as a thought, check it once against the evidence, and then act in line with what matters to you. The toolkit page has a thought check worksheet to help.

Key ideas

  • Fear grows when danger feels high and your ability to cope feels low. Both can be checked.
  • Catastrophising, overestimating odds, and mind reading are common traps.
  • Intolerance of uncertainty sits underneath many forms of anxiety.
  • Avoidance brings quick relief and keeps fear alive. Safety behaviours do the same thing more quietly.

Try this: Thought check

  1. Write down the fearful thought exactly as it appears.
  2. What is the evidence for it? What is the evidence against it?
  3. If it did happen, how would you cope? Who could help?
  4. What would you say to a friend who had this thought?
  5. Write a more balanced version, and one small action you will take anyway.

More worksheets live in the practice toolkit.

Reflect

What do you do to feel certain when you are anxious? What might happen if you let a little uncertainty stay?
