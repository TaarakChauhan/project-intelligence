Courage as a practice

Values, self compassion, sleep, movement, and when to reach out for help.

Courage is not the absence of fear. It is acting on what matters while fear is still present. That is good news, because it means you do not have to wait to feel ready. Courage is a skill, and like any skill, it grows with practice.

Choose what matters

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, often called ACT, starts from a simple idea. Trying hard to get rid of fear often makes life smaller. Instead, you can make room for the feeling and take steps toward your values anyway. Reviews of many studies show ACT helps with anxiety and other problems, with effects broadly similar to other established therapies.

Values are not goals. A goal is something you finish. A value is a direction, like being honest, being creative, or caring for family. When fear shows up, you can ask: what would someone who holds this value do next?

Be kind to yourself

Many people believe that being harsh with themselves keeps them sharp. Research suggests the opposite. In studies by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen, people who were encouraged to be kind to themselves after a failure spent more effort trying to improve than those who were simply encouraged to feel good about themselves.

Self compassion makes failure less frightening, and when failure is less frightening, courage is easier.

Look after the body

Fear lives in the body, so the body is part of the answer. Sleep loss makes the brain more reactive to threat and weakens the regulating role of the prefrontal cortex. Research from Berkeley found that a night without sleep raised next day anxiety, and that deep sleep helped restore calm.

Physical activity helps too. A large review of reviews in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that exercise reduced anxiety symptoms by a meaningful amount, across many kinds of activity. Walking counts.

Small acts, repeated

Courage grows through small acts done often. Each time you face something you feared and come through it, you collect evidence that you can cope. Over time, that evidence becomes confidence.

Keep a courage log. One line a day. What you did, what you feared, what happened. Read it when fear tells you that you cannot.

When to get help

Self help tools are useful, but they are not a replacement for care. Cognitive behavioural therapy, often with exposure, is among the most effective treatments for anxiety disorders. Medication can also help some people. A family doctor is a good place to start.

Please reach out to a doctor or mental health professional if fear or anxiety is overwhelming, lasts for weeks, or stops you living the way you want. In Canada, you can call or text 988 at any time if you are in crisis or thinking about suicide. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

Key ideas

  • Courage means acting on what matters while fear is still present.
  • ACT helps you make room for fear and move toward your values.
  • Self compassion after failure increases the motivation to improve.
  • Sleep and exercise both lower anxiety and support emotional regulation.
  • Professional help works. Reaching out is a courageous act.

Try this: Values compass

  1. Write down three values that matter most to you.
  2. For each one, write one small action you could take this week that fear has been stopping.
  3. Pick one. Do it, even with the fear present.
  4. Add a line to your courage log: what you did, what you feared, what happened.

Reflect

Imagine yourself a year from now, having acted with courage on what matters. What did you do that you are proud of?
