Fight, flight, freeze, and fawn

The four ways people react under threat, and why none of them is a character flaw.

Most people know the phrase fight or flight. The full picture is wider. Under threat, people and animals move through a set of responses, and the one that shows up depends on the situation, your history, and what seems most likely to keep you safe.

The defence cascade

Clinicians describe a sequence of defensive states, sometimes called the defence cascade. It begins with arousal, a sudden alert stillness while you work out what is happening. Then comes fight or flight if action seems possible.

Freeze is often misunderstood. In the research, freeze is not giving up. It is an alert, tense stillness, like a deer that stops moving so it will not be seen. When escape seems impossible, some people go into a deeper shutdown, where the body goes limp or numb. That is a protective reflex, not a choice.

Where fawn fits

Fawn is a newer term from trauma therapy. It describes calming a threat by pleasing it, agreeing quickly, apologising, or putting the other person's needs first. It is useful language, and many people recognise themselves in it, but it was not part of the original research model and has been studied less than the others.

In daily life fawn can look like saying yes to avoid conflict, laughing off a comment that hurt, or changing your opinion the moment someone frowns.

Modern versions

  • Fight: snapping at a colleague, arguing harder, getting defensive in feedback.
  • Flight: avoiding the email, leaving early, changing the subject, procrastinating.
  • Freeze: going blank in a meeting, staring at the screen, not being able to decide.
  • Fawn: over agreeing, over apologising, taking on extra work to keep the peace.

Why it is not your fault, and what you can do

These responses happen fast and below conscious choice. Blaming yourself for freezing in a tough moment is like blaming yourself for blinking when something flies at your face.

What you can do is learn your pattern. Once you notice it early, you can buy a few seconds, steady your body, and choose a response that fits the real situation rather than the imagined one.

Key ideas

  • Under threat, people move through arousal, fight, flight, freeze, and sometimes shutdown.
  • Freeze is alert stillness. Shutdown is a deeper protective reflex when escape seems impossible.
  • Fawn means calming a threat by pleasing it. It is a helpful clinical idea but less studied.
  • These reactions are automatic. You are not to blame for them, but you can learn to spot them.

Try this: Find your default

  1. Recall three recent moments of conflict or pressure.
  2. For each, write what you did in the first ten seconds.
  3. Label it fight, flight, freeze, or fawn.
  4. Write one small move you could try next time, such as a slow breath, asking for a minute, or saying let me think about that.

Reflect

Which response did you learn early in life, and who or what did it protect you from? Is it still serving you today?
