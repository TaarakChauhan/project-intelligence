The practice toolkit

Ten tools, from a sixty second reset to a full fear ladder. Use the quick ones in the moment and the worksheets once a week. This page prints cleanly on white paper.

In the moment

1. Slow exhale breathing

Use it when: your heart is racing or your chest feels tight.

  1. Breathe in through your nose.
  2. Take a second short breath in on top of the first, to fill the lungs.
  3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth, making the exhale longer than the inhale.
  4. Repeat for one to five minutes.

Why it helps: A long exhale engages the calming side of the nervous system. In a 2023 study, five minutes a day of this kind of breathing improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation alone.

Evidence: Cell Reports Medicine, via PMC, Harvard Health Publishing

2. Name it to tame it

Use it when: fear feels big and blurry.

  1. Pause and say, silently or out loud, what you feel. I feel afraid. I feel anxious about tomorrow.
  2. Be specific if you can. Embarrassed, dread, nervous, panicky.
  3. Notice that you are the one noticing the feeling. You are not the feeling.

Why it helps: Putting feelings into words has been linked with lower activity in the amygdala and more activity in regulating parts of the prefrontal cortex.

Evidence: Psychological Science, via PubMed

3. Reframe the surge

Use it when: you are about to perform, speak, or compete.

  1. Notice the racing heart and the energy.
  2. Instead of telling yourself to calm down, say I am excited.
  3. Point the energy at the task. What do I want this audience to get?

Why it helps: Fear and excitement feel similar in the body. Research by Alison Wood Brooks found that people who said I am excited before singing, speaking, or a maths task performed better than those who tried to calm down. More broadly, reappraising a situation tends to work better than suppressing the feeling.

Evidence: Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, via PubMed, Psychophysiology

4. Five senses grounding

Use it when: you feel swept up, unreal, or on the edge of panic.

  1. Name five things you can see.
  2. Four things you can feel, like your feet on the floor.
  3. Three things you can hear.
  4. Two things you can smell.
  5. One thing you can taste, or one slow breath.

Why it helps: Grounding moves attention out of the threat loop and back into the present moment, where you are usually safe. It is a common tool in anxiety care.

Evidence: NHS

6. Fear ladder

Use it when: you want to face a fear in steps, such as speaking up, driving, flying, or making calls.

  1. Write your goal at the top, the thing you want to be able to do.
  2. List eight smaller steps toward it, from easiest to hardest.
  3. Rate how scary each step feels from 0 to 100.
  4. Start with a step around 30 to 40. Stay with it until you learn something new, rather than leaving as soon as fear rises.
  5. Repeat each step a few times and in different places before moving up. Drop your safety behaviours as you go.

7. Prediction log

Use it when: you do a step on your ladder. It turns each step into an experiment.

What I will doWhat I predict will happen, and how sure I amWhat actually happenedWhat I learned

Why it helps: exposure seems to work best when reality surprises your prediction. Writing the prediction down first makes the surprise easy to see.

Evidence: Craske and colleagues, Bouton, Maren and McNally.

8. Worry window

Use it when: worries follow you all day or keep you up at night.

  1. Choose a fifteen minute window each day, at the same time and place, not close to bedtime.
  2. When a worry shows up outside that window, write it down in a word or two and tell yourself, later.
  3. During the window, go through the list. For each worry, ask: can I act on this? If yes, choose one step. If no, practise letting it be uncertain.
  4. When the time ends, stop, even if you have not finished.

Why it helps: Worry often feels urgent and endless. Giving it a set time teaches you that it can wait, and that you can live with some uncertainty. This is a common technique in cognitive behavioural therapy for worry.

Evidence: Journal of Anxiety Disorders, NHS

10. Courage log

Use it: every day, one line. Read it back when fear says you cannot.

DateWhat I didWhat I fearedWhat happened

Be kind to yourself on the days it goes badly. Self compassion after a setback makes people more likely to try again, not less.

Evidence: Breines and Chen.

The daily basics

  • Sleep. Aim for a regular bedtime. Sleep loss makes the brain more reactive to threat the next day.
  • Move. Walking, lifting, dancing, or any activity you enjoy. Regular exercise reduces anxiety symptoms.
  • Connect. Fear shrinks when it is shared. Tell one person what you are working on.
  • Go easy on caffeine and alcohol when you feel anxious. Both can make the body feel more alarmed.

Evidence: Ben Simon and colleagues, Singh and colleagues, NHS.
