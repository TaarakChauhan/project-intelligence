## 1. Collection overview

This library is a practical decision system for people who allocate capital, run companies, advise boards, or teach business judgment. It contains **approximately 50 catalogue files** (the machine-readable manifest lists 50 entries), arranged as a progression from general reasoning to specialist applications. The purpose is not to produce certainty. It is to make assumptions visible, match effort to stakes, preserve optionality where possible, and create a record from which people can learn.

The library is for:

- **Venture capitalists and aspiring investors**, who must underwrite uncertain companies in a portfolio context.
- **Angel investors**, who invest personal capital with different liquidity, concentration, time, and emotional constraints.
- **Owners and founders**, who make repeated choices about people, pricing, product, cash, financing, and survival.
- **Board members and governance learners**, who need informed oversight without becoming shadow management.
- **Teachers, facilitators, and teams**, who want a common vocabulary and exercises rather than a lecture full of slogans.

It was built through public web research and original teaching synthesis. The lessons cite the underlying material, including Annie Duke’s work on separating decision quality from outcomes, Amazon shareholder letters on reversible decisions, Gary Klein’s premortem, OODA material, Kahneman/Lovallo/Sibony on decision traps, the Gompers–Gornall–Kaplan–Strebulaev VC survey, YC financing documents, Canadian and Delaware governance sources, and practical investor/operator material. The prose here compresses and connects those lessons; it does not reproduce them as a substitute for the linked sources or professional advice.

## 2. The shared decision operating system

The library’s central idea is simple: **judge decisions by the quality of the process and information available at the time, not only by the result**. A good decision can lose because the world is noisy; a weak decision can win because of luck. Annie Duke’s “thinking in bets” framing is useful because it turns certainty into explicit beliefs: what do we think will happen, how confident are we, what evidence supports that belief, and what would change it? At review, do not rewrite the original forecast to fit the outcome. Separate process quality from outcome quality and ask, “What did we know then?”

That discipline prevents outcome bias and hindsight bias. If a retailer’s new location fails after an unforeseen road closure, the loss is evidence to investigate, not proof that the original decision was foolish. Conversely, a lucky win does not excuse weak research, no alternatives, or no review trigger. The objective is a better controllable loop: clearer framing, better base rates, disconfirming evidence, proportionate process, and honest learning.

### Match ceremony to reversibility

Borrow the one-way/two-way-door distinction associated with Bezos’s shareholder-letter framing. A **two-way door** can be reversed at tolerable cost: run a bounded experiment, appoint one owner, set a deadline, and measure. A landing-page change or a small regional pilot usually belongs here. A **one-way door** is difficult to undo or carries asymmetric harm: signing an exclusive five-year contract, taking on material debt, changing control, or making a reputational or regulatory commitment. Slow down enough to test assumptions, invite dissent, model downside, and obtain the approvals the authority structure requires.

Reversibility is contextual. A technically reversible product toggle may not be reputationally reversible after customers lose trust. Every commitment should therefore name its rollback, adjustment, or kill trigger before the team becomes emotionally attached. Mixed decisions can often be decomposed: make a smaller reversible move that buys information before making the irreversible one.

### A small mental-model toolkit

Use models as lenses, not as decoration. Three useful lenses are usually better than ten:

- **Expected value (EV):** compare probability-weighted outcomes across a base, upside, and downside case. Show ranges and assumptions. EV supports comparison; it is not a promise.
- **Inversion:** ask how the plan could fail, then remove the most preventable causes. A premortem makes this social: imagine the project has failed, list plausible causes, and identify leading indicators.
- **Second-order thinking:** trace what happens after the first effect, including incentives, customer response, competitor response, and internal behaviour. A metric or term can create a behaviour that defeats the original objective.
- **OODA:** observe, orient, decide, act. In fast-moving settings, shorten the learning loop rather than waiting for a perfect forecast.
- **Regret minimisation:** consider what a future self could explain, including the cost of inaction. This is a comparison tool, not a licence to rationalise fear.
- **Margin of safety:** leave room for forecast error, cash shocks, delays, churn, and execution slippage. It is especially important where downside is hard to reverse.

A useful example is a second sales channel. EV compares incremental contribution, implementation cost, and cannibalisation; inversion tests channel conflict and partner trust; OODA suggests a regional pilot with weekly observation. The model earns its place only if it can change the plan.

### Journals, calibration, and bias safeguards

A decision journal is a small dataset, not a diary. Before acting, record the date, owner, deadline, authority, objective, constraints, alternatives (including do nothing), known facts, assumptions, base/upside/downside cases, rough probabilities, disconfirming evidence, and what would change your mind. Record leading indicators, a stop/adjust/escalate trigger, and a review date. At review, compare predictions with actuals and distinguish luck from process.

Calibration is a long-run property. If a forecaster assigns 70% to comparable events, those events should occur roughly 70% of the time across a set; a single miss is not automatically incompetence. Avoid false precision such as 63% when the evidence cannot support it. Use ranges, reference classes, and independent estimates.

Design against predictable traps:

- **Confirmation:** assign someone to find disconfirming evidence.
- **Anchoring:** collect independent estimates before sharing the first number.
- **Availability:** use base rates, not only vivid examples.
- **Sunk cost:** compare the next dollar and hour, not what has already been spent.
- **Overconfidence:** use ranges and outside views.
- **Groupthink and authority bias:** gather written first views before the senior voice speaks.
- **Action bias:** recognise that waiting for a defined signal can be an active choice.

Bias is a reason to improve the environment, not a character diagnosis. A red team needs time and permission to matter; a premortem must lead to mitigations; a pass log should be learned from rather than treated as wasted effort.

### The operating loop

Use the loop **Frame → options → evidence → decision rights → commit → observe → review**.

1. **Frame:** State the decision in one sentence; name the objective, constraints, owner, deadline, and do-nothing path. Label it one-way, two-way, or mixed.
2. **Options:** Include a preferred path, alternatives, a smaller test, and waiting. Do not confuse a favourite with the only option.
3. **Evidence:** Identify the claims that must be true, the evidence that would update belief, the source and quality of that evidence, and the downside if wrong.
4. **Decision rights:** Name who recommends, contributes, decides, executes, approves, and reviews. More voices do not equal more accountability.
5. **Commit:** State the decision, rationale, boundaries, next action, and dissent heard. Disagree-and-commit means dissent before the call, not silence after it.
6. **Observe:** Track leading indicators and cash/customer/people signals; use a small feedback loop for uncertain decisions.
7. **Review:** On the scheduled date, assess process, forecast misses, luck, and one improvement. Reopen only with new evidence or a pre-defined trigger.

### The first 30 minutes

The `START-HERE.md` orientation turns the loop into a half-hour practice:

- **Minutes 0–5:** frame the decision, owner, deadline, objective, constraints, do-nothing option, and door type.
- **Minutes 5–10:** list three assumptions, base/upside/downside cases, and the evidence that would change your mind.
- **Minutes 10–15:** run inversion, a premortem, and second-order analysis.
- **Minutes 15–20:** choose research, voices, decision authority, and a review trigger.
- **Minutes 20–25:** record the decision and rough probabilities in the journal.
- **Minutes 25–30:** schedule review and define leading indicators.

This is deliberately lightweight. Add a memo, specialist review, board or investment committee process only when stakes, uncertainty, irreversibility, or authority justify it.

## 3. How venture capitalists decide

VC is portfolio underwriting under uncertainty, not a product beauty contest. Returns are unevenly distributed, so a fund asks both “Is this a good company?” and “Can this become a fund-relevant outcome at this price, ownership, stage, and reserve plan?” The answer depends on mandate, portfolio construction, concentration, follow-on capacity, conflicts, and partner conviction.

The strongest quantitative anchor in this collection is Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan, and Strebulaev’s survey of **885 VCs at 681 firms**. Approximately **95%** mentioned team as important and approximately **47%** called team the most important factor. Business model was mentioned by approximately **83%**, product by approximately **74%**, and market by approximately **68%**. Deal selection ranked as the most important among sourcing, selection, and value-add activities. These are survey findings, not a mechanical scorecard: a fund’s strategy can make a rational pass on a company another fund should pursue.

### Funnel and stage gates

A typical funnel is **screen → deep dive → partner review → memo → investment committee (IC)**. At screen, test mandate fit, stage, founder contact, problem, and initial evidence. In a deep dive, test the thesis across customers, product, market, team, economics, legal, and material ESG or reputation risk. Partner review should include a sponsor and a credible challenger. The memo should make facts, claims, assumptions, terms, scenarios, and open items legible. IC should decide approve, pass, dated defer, or change terms.

Every gate needs a question, evidence requirement, owner, and stop/defer rule. This prevents diligence theatre and sunk-cost escalation. A pass log turns “no” decisions into learning about mandate, evidence, and red flags. Circulate memos early enough for independent views and record conflicts and abstentions.

### Diligence domains

Start with the investment thesis and list what must be true. Then triangulate with primary evidence:

- **Financial:** reconcile revenue, bookings, billings, cash, burn, runway, liabilities, forecasts, margins, and cohorts.
- **Commercial/market:** define ICP, buyer, pain, timing, budget, alternatives, competition, distribution, and a transparent TAM/SAM/SOM method.
- **Product/technology:** observe activation, usage, retention, expansion, roadmap, architecture, security, IP, and technical feasibility.
- **Team:** assess founder-market fit, roles, complementarity, hiring gaps, conflict handling, speed, and response to bad news.
- **Legal:** scope incorporation, IP assignment, employment, material contracts, privacy, regulation, financing history, and authority.
- **ESG/reputation:** identify safety, environmental, people, compliance, customer concentration, and reputational risks that could affect the thesis.

For every material claim, record supporting evidence, contradicting evidence, confidence, downside if false, and the next test. Ask for source-system data, bank or accounting reconciliation, contracts, customer calls, code or architecture review, and independent references where appropriate. Protect confidential information and use specialists for specialist questions.

### Evidence changes by stage

- **Pre-seed:** vision, problem intensity, founder insight, technical feasibility, early tests, and learning velocity. Mature LTV/CAC is usually premature, but basic ownership and IP hygiene are not.
- **Seed:** demand evidence, early retention or usage, customer discovery, initial distribution, and a credible next milestone. Ask what risk this round will retire.
- **Series A:** repeatable acquisition and retention, coherent unit-economic direction, scalable team, and a model for the next phase. A large pilot is not repeatable demand.
- **Growth:** durable cohorts, efficient growth, forecast reliability, governance, financing independence or a path to profit, and market structure.

The “jockey versus horse” shorthand is incomplete. Team and market interact: a strong team in a tiny market may not fit a venture mandate, while a large market cannot compensate for weak execution. Look for earned insight, customer access, credibility, speed of learning, resilience, and behaviour when data is bad—not only pedigree or charisma. Product evidence should be behaviour, not only a demo.

### Unit economics and capital efficiency

Treat LTV/CAC, payback, burn multiple, and runway as models. Define revenue, gross margin, contribution margin, CAC, retention, expansion, churn, and period consistently. LTV/CAC is sensitive to cohort, gross margin, contract length, expansion, and churn assumptions. Burn multiple can be expressed as net cash burned divided by net new recurring revenue, but the numerator, denominator, and period must be stated. Runway is cash available divided by defined monthly net burn; scenario cash flow matters more than a single number.

Reconcile metrics to cash and accounting records. Separate one-off contracts, pilots, founder-led sales, subsidies, and working-capital effects. Model downside when CAC rises, churn worsens, a hire slips, or a financing is delayed. Capital efficiency is not spending as little as possible; it is buying learning and durable growth at a risk-appropriate cost.

### Red flags and memo anatomy

Red flags are prompts for investigation, not automatic proof of failure. Common examples include inconsistent metrics, weak or unverified retention, overstated TAM, customer concentration, undisclosed liabilities, missing IP assignment, founder conflict, privacy or regulatory gaps, and integrity concerns. Classify each as known, bounded, mitigated, unresolved, or disqualifying under the mandate. Pre-commit pass criteria before advocacy pressure rises.

A brief investment memo should contain: decision requested (amount, instrument, ownership, conditions, reserves); thesis; verified facts versus management claims versus assumptions; market and customer; product and technology; team and references; economics; legal/ESG; base/upside/downside; terms and portfolio fit; three material risks with mitigants and triggers; recommendation to approve, pass, or defer; dissent and open items. The memo is a falsifiable decision record, not a sales document.

## 4. How angel investors decide

An angel uses personal capital, so the lens differs from a VC fund. Personal liquidity, concentration, taxes, time, relationships, and emotional stakes matter. An angel may invest for financial return, learning, community, strategic fit, or access, but must name the objective and set a written mandate: cheque range, stage/sector, annual loss budget, time budget, concentration limit, and follow-on policy.

The five core checks are:

1. **Founder-market fit:** why this team, for this problem, now?
2. **Timing and wedge:** who buys first, what changed, and how can the beachhead expand?
3. **Model integrity:** do metrics reconcile to customers, contracts, cohorts, cash, and the cap table?
4. **References and momentum:** is learning or adoption accelerating, and can it be independently verified?
5. **Terms and fit:** do the SAFE, note, or priced-round terms, rights, ownership, and follow-on expectations fit the portfolio?

Founder-market fit means earned insight, customer access, relevant craft, and learning speed—not merely a prestigious résumé or an emotional origin story. Ask for a customer workflow, a failed experiment, an overlooked constraint, and the next falsifiable test. Speak to a customer or expert independently and explore co-founder complementarity and conflict handling.

A light diligence playbook can be a 48-hour screen followed by a one-week deeper pass if the thesis survives. Verify identity, incorporation, ownership, IP assignment, material contracts, liabilities, customer claims, financing history, and instrument terms as appropriate. Source at least one reference independently; seek a churned or non-buying perspective where possible. Use a red/amber/green table for ownership, cash, customers, IP, team, terms, and compliance. “Standard terms” are not consequence-free.

Size a cheque from the amount that can be lost without changing essential life plans. Reserve follow-on capital only if strategy and access justify it. Model total loss, dilution, time to liquidity, currency, taxes, future rounds, SAFE cap/discount, and pro-rata exposure. Diversification reduces single-deal pressure but does not guarantee a return; early-stage investments are illiquid and highly uncertain. Portfolio thinking should stop friendship, fear of missing out, or sunk-cost averaging from overriding the written mandate.

## 5. How business owners and founders decide

An owner’s decision operating system makes recurring choices visible, owned, and reviewable. Maintain a weekly decision queue. For every item, write the one-sentence question, stakes, reversibility, urgency, uncertainty, decision owner, contributors, deadline, evidence needed, next action, leading indicators, and kill/adjust trigger. Use an experiment for a two-way door and a short memo for a one-way door. Escalate only decisions that exceed delegated authority.

For common allocation decisions:

- **Hiring:** define the bottleneck, expected outcomes, ramp time, manager capacity, and a contractor or test alternative. Do not hire merely to relieve discomfort.
- **Pricing:** test willingness to pay and value delivered by segment; include implementation cost, margin, communication, and customer trust. Competitor menus are not a value model.
- **Build versus buy:** compare total cost, speed, differentiation, lock-in, security, integration, maintenance, and capability. “Buy” is not cheap if switching costs are hidden.
- **Pivot:** identify which assumption failed, what evidence supports the new direction, what assets transfer, and what would stop or narrow the pivot. A new feature is not automatically a pivot.

Raising capital is a strategic decision, not a validation ceremony. Compare bootstrapping, debt, equity, grants, and waiting by milestone purchased, dilution, control, repayment, covenants, reporting, investor fit, speed, and downside. Build a 12–24 month cash plan with scenarios. Raise before a runway cliff, but avoid selling too much uncertainty if a measurable milestone is near. Include the do-not-raise plan and the costs of stopping, delaying, or learning.

Cash survival decisions deserve earlier action than panic. Run a rolling forecast with base, downside, and severe cases, including collections timing, working capital, debt service, taxes, commitments, and seasonal effects. Set pre-agreed triggers for hiring pauses, vendor renegotiation, pricing action, financing outreach, or wind-down advice. A financing hope is not cash on hand. If obligations may not be met, obtain qualified accounting, tax, insolvency, and legal advice.

Disagree-and-commit works only with real dissent before the call, one accountable decider, boundaries, and a review trigger. Use a RACI-like map: recommend, input, decide, execute, review. After the decision, communicate what commitment means and reopen only for new evidence or the defined trigger.

## 6. Boards and governance (educational overview)

Directors generally owe duties to the corporation, not simply to the investor or person who nominated them. **Care** means being informed, attentive, and appropriately supervised. **Loyalty** means good faith, managing conflicts, and not misusing corporate opportunities. The details depend on the entity, governing statute, charter, agreements, case law, and jurisdiction.

The board governs purpose, CEO oversight, strategy and risk oversight, major capital and structural decisions, and accountability. Management executes within delegated authority and reports honestly. A decision-rights schedule should define reserved matters, budgets, signing authority, hiring authority, financing thresholds, and escalation triggers. Boards should challenge assumptions and outcomes without becoming a shadow operating team; information rights are not operating authority.

The business judgment rule is a broad educational concept, not immunity. In jurisdictions such as Delaware, informed, good-faith, disinterested decisions within authority may receive deference, subject to doctrine and facts. It does not protect fraud, bad faith, illegality, conflicts, or an inadequate process. Courts and statutes differ. Canada and Delaware are not interchangeable: Canadian corporate duties and remedies must be analysed under the applicable Canadian statute, articles, agreements, and jurisprudence; a Delaware formulation cannot be copied automatically.

A conflicted transaction may involve financial, relational, role-based, or informational interests. Disclose early. Clarify who can receive information, negotiate, participate, vote, or abstain. Use independent directors or advisers where appropriate, test alternatives and fairness, and document disclosure, evidence, approvals, and rationale. Do not backfill a process after closing. **This section is not legal advice; ask local counsel about an actual transaction or duty.**

## 7. Deal terms and capital (conceptual)

A **SAFE** is a contract for future equity subject to its text; it is not ordinary shares today and is not automatically “free” or dilution-free. A **convertible note** is debt that may convert and can include maturity, interest, priority, and other obligations. A **priced round** issues shares at a negotiated price and commonly brings fuller governance and investor rights. Cap, discount, conversion mechanics, pro-rata, information rights, seniority, side letters, and amendments can change the result. Read the actual documents with securities counsel in the relevant jurisdiction.

A cap table is the ownership ledger: legal holders, issued shares, options, SAFEs, notes, conversion assumptions, and fully diluted percentages. Dilution means an existing holder owns a smaller percentage after new shares or conversion; value can still rise if capital creates more enterprise value. Model pre-money/post-money definitions, option-pool treatment, conversion scenarios, future rounds, and pro-rata. Ownership percentage is not the same as control or exit proceeds.

Liquidation preferences determine priority in an exit waterfall. Model low, middle, and high exits and identify multiple, participating/non-participating treatment, seniority, conversion, dividends, and carve-outs. A holder with 20% common-equivalent and a 1× non-participating preference may choose the preference at a low exit and conversion at a high exit; participating treatment produces a different waterfall. The charter and financing documents control.

Terms are relationship design. Translate each term into “who benefits, when, and at whose cost?” A higher valuation may come with aggressive control or preference; a lower valuation may come with transparent governance and useful reserves. Test up-round, flat/down-round, low-exit, and founder-departure scenarios. Discuss board composition, protective provisions, pro-rata, information, and vesting with advisers. Alignment also shows in behaviour: transparency, handling bad news, decision rights, and follow-on expectations.

## 9. Learning paths

The collection has four role-based paths, each estimated at roughly **four weeks of light study and practice** (the manifest estimates 20 minutes for each path file; the deliverables require applying the lessons).

- **Aspiring investor:** Week 1 foundations and probabilities; Week 2 VC lens, funnel, and stage risk; Week 3 team/market/product, economics, red flags, and memo; Week 4 angel checks and terms. Deliver an investment memo, a pass memo, and a calibration review.
- **Founder raising:** Begin with foundations, then VC stage evidence and funnel; prepare metrics, red flags, memo narrative, runway and financing comparisons; finish with the premortem and cap-table worksheet with counsel. Deliver a use-of-proceeds plan, downside runway plan, evidence room, and investor questions.
- **Operating owner:** Foundations, owner lessons, and four weeks of weekly reviews; add terms if ownership or financing matters. Deliver a decision queue, three explicit triggers, and a post-decision review.
- **Board member:** Foundations, governance lessons, terms, and a hypothetical decision record/conflict protocol. Deliver a decision-rights schedule and board agenda that surfaces choices, evidence, and conflicts; seek local counsel for real duties.

## 10. Teaching others

The 90-minute workshop is designed for a mixed group. Spend 0–10 minutes on outcome bias and one lucky win/process miss; 10–25 framing a live decision in the journal; 25–40 classifying doors and designing a reversible test; 40–55 applying EV, inversion, and a premortem; 55–70 using the investor lens of stage evidence, diligence, red flags, and memo; 70–82 practising governance, conflicts, and escalation; and 82–90 making an individual commitment with a review date.

Discussion prompts ask when a bad outcome is process evidence versus noise, what makes a decision irreversible, which assumption is fragile, what a churned customer would say, when TAM distracts, which metric creates a dangerous incentive, what the board should know versus decide, how a conflicted director should participate, which term changes downside behaviour, and what would make someone pass on a good opportunity. Facilitation should invite dissent, distinguish facts from claims, and end with a test or decision owner.

The quiz bank closes a session with checks such as: whether a good outcome proves a good decision (no); what a two-way door is; how a premortem works; what a memo must distinguish; how stage evidence shifts; why blended LTV/CAC misleads; the five angel checks; whether a SAFE is equity today (no); the board/management boundary; limits of the business judgment rule; what runway triggers do; and why passes should be recorded.

## 11. Glossary highlights

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|

| Two-way / one-way door | Reversible-at-tolerable-cost choice / hard-to-reverse or asymmetric-risk choice |
| EV | Probability-weighted value across possible outcomes |
| Premortem | Imagine failure has happened and identify causes and preventions |
| Calibration | Whether probabilities match frequencies across comparable forecasts |
| Due diligence | Proportionate investigation of claims, risks, evidence, and terms |
| Founder-market fit | Earned insight, access, credibility, and stamina for a problem and market |
| PMF | Repeated evidence that a defined market values, adopts, retains, and ideally pays |
| TAM / SAM / SOM | Total addressable / serviceable available / serviceable obtainable market |
| LTV/CAC | Modelled customer lifetime value divided by acquisition cost; assumptions matter |
| Burn multiple | Defined net cash burn divided by defined net new recurring revenue |
| Runway | Cash available divided by defined monthly net burn, with scenario limits |
| Cap table | Ownership ledger including shares, options, SAFEs/notes, and assumptions |
| SAFE | Simple Agreement for Future Equity, subject to its exact contract |
| Liquidation preference | Priority claim on proceeds before common holders, as documented |
| Pro-rata right | Negotiated right, subject to conditions, to participate in future financing |
| Fiduciary duty | Defined duty of trust, care, and/or loyalty; entity and jurisdiction matter |
| Business judgment rule | Possible deference to informed, good-faith, disinterested decisions, not immunity |
| Value-add | Specific investor support backed by credible capacity and references |

## 13. How to use this collection tomorrow

1. Open this master document first, then read `START-HERE.md` if you want the compact orientation.
2. Pick one live decision—not an abstract case—and write it in one sentence.
3. Classify it as one-way, two-way, or mixed; add a smaller test if possible.
4. Name the owner, deadline, authority, alternatives, do-nothing path, and review date.
5. Write three assumptions and one piece of disconfirming evidence you will actively seek.
6. Choose no more than three models: EV, inversion/premortem, second-order, OODA, regret, or margin of safety.
7. Use the role-specific worksheet: journal, VC checklist, angel scorecard, founder premortem, memo outline, or weekly review.
8. Make the decision, communicate it, and state the trigger that reopens it.
9. Review the process on the date you wrote down; do not wait for a dramatic win or failure.
10. If the decision involves securities, fiduciary duties, tax, accounting, insolvency, privacy, employment, or a material contract, move from education to qualified local advice.

## Appendix B — Suggested reading order

### Day 1

Read this master document through Sections 1–3, then complete the six-line decision journal for one live decision. Finish the `START-HERE.md` 30-minute orientation and schedule a review date.

### Week 1

Read all five foundation lessons. Use the one-way/two-way-door exercise, write three probability forecasts, and run a premortem. Begin a small journal rather than waiting for a major decision.

### Role-specific

- **VC or aspiring investor:** Read `02-venture-capital/` in order, complete the diligence checklist, write an approve memo and a pass memo, then read angel and terms material.
- **Angel:** Read the angel folder, score a hypothetical deal, run light diligence, and model cheque, reserve, cap-table, and downside exposure.
- **Founder raising:** Read VC stages and funnel to understand the questions coming; then read owner runway/raising lessons and the terms folder. Build a use-of-proceeds plan and a downside cash plan with advisers.
- **Operating owner:** Read the owner folder, adopt the weekly review for four weeks, and add foundations whenever a decision is emotionally loaded or difficult to reverse.
- **Board member:** Read foundations 01, 03, and 05; then boards and governance and terms. Draft decision rights, a conflict protocol, and an agenda that highlights choices rather than status.
- **Teacher or facilitator:** Use the 90-minute workshop, discussion prompts, and quiz bank. Ask learners to bring a real decision and leave with an owner and review date.
