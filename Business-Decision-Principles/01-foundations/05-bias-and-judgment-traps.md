# Bias and judgment traps

**Purpose:** Detect predictable errors before they become expensive commitments.

**Who this is for:** Any team making hiring, investment, strategy, or financing decisions.

## Core principles
- **Confirmation:** seek a disconfirming test and give someone the job of finding it.
- **Anchoring:** collect independent estimates before sharing the first number.
- **Availability:** use base rates, not only vivid examples.
- **Sunk cost:** compare the next dollar and hour, not what has already been spent.
- **Overconfidence:** use ranges, reference classes, and outside views.
- **Groupthink and authority bias:** gather written views before the senior voice speaks.
- **Action bias:** sometimes waiting for a defined signal is the active choice.

## How decisions actually get made
Build small process safeguards: independent first passes, red-team reviews, premortems, kill criteria, and a “what would change my mind?” field. Bias is not a character flaw; it is a reason to design better decision environments.

## Worked mini-example
A founder wants to keep a failing feature because “we are too far in.” A sunk-cost check asks whether a new team would start it today, while a customer cohort review tests whether any segment retains it. The answer may be to stop, narrow, or continue—but now for current evidence.

## Common mistakes
- Naming a bias as a substitute for analysis.
- Assuming the other side is biased while your side is objective.
- Adding a red team with no authority or time.
- Treating speed as proof of decisiveness.

## Practice exercise (7 minutes)
Choose a live decision. Write the strongest case for your preferred option, then write the strongest disconfirming evidence. Ask one person to challenge only the assumptions, not your identity.
