# Business judgment rule basics

**Purpose:** Understand the idea of deference to proper corporate decisions without treating it as immunity.

**Who this is for:** Board members, founders, investors, and students of corporate governance.

## Core principles
- In broad terms, the business judgment rule can create a presumption/deference for informed, good-faith, disinterested decisions made within authority; doctrine varies by jurisdiction.
- It does not protect fraud, bad faith, conflicts, illegality, or a process so deficient that the relevant standard is not met.
- Courts generally assess process and loyalty, not whether they would have made the business choice.
- Conflicts require a different process; independent approval and disclosure may matter under applicable law.

## How decisions actually get made
Ask whether directors had adequate information, time, expert advice where needed, honest deliberation, and a rational connection between evidence and action. Minutes should record material facts and discussion, not advocacy theatre. Never use this lesson as a defence strategy.

## Worked mini-example
A board approves a product investment after reviewing scenarios, customer evidence, cash impact, and specialist advice. The project later fails. Failure alone does not prove a bad process; the record and facts determine the analysis.

## Common mistakes
- Saying “BJR” ends a conversation.
- Confusing an informed risk with negligence or bad faith.
- Treating conflicted approval as ordinary approval.
- Assuming one jurisdiction’s formulation applies everywhere.

## Practice exercise (8 minutes)
Review a hypothetical decision and mark informed facts, missing facts, conflicts, advice, authority, and review. Identify what a lawyer should verify.
