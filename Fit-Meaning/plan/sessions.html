Curriculum

Five sessions

~20–30 minutes each · do them in order

Five sessions. Do them in order. Each is designed for about 20–30 minutes: read, do one short activity, then check that you actually got it.

Do not binge all five in one sitting if the ideas start to blur. The whole point is to replace a slogan with a mechanism you can use.

Session 1 — The phrase is not the idea (20–25 min)

Goal. Be able to say, in your own words, what biologists mean by fitness, and why that is not "the strongest survive."

Read. Start here (if you have not) and Lesson 1. Skim the glossary entries for fitness, natural selection, and adaptation.

Activity (8 minutes). Write four sentences, no notes:

  1. Who coined "survival of the fittest," and how Darwin used it.
  2. The scientific meaning of fitness.
  3. One everyday use of the phrase that gets fitness wrong.
  4. Why fitness is always relative to an environment.

You got it if you can explain why a slow, well-camouflaged animal can be fitter than a fast, conspicuous one — without falling back on "because nature wanted it that way."

Session 2 — The three-part mechanism (25–30 min)

Goal. Recite the logic of natural selection without examples, then apply it to one example with every step named.

Read. Lesson 2. Glossary: variation, heritability, phenotype, genotype, mutation.

Activity (10 minutes). Pick one of: peppered moths, antibiotic resistance, or Darwin's finches. On paper, fill in:

  • What varied?
  • Was the variation heritable? How do you know, at the level this lesson gives you?
  • Who reproduced more, and why, in that environment?
  • What changed in the population afterward?
  • What would you expect if the environment flipped?

You got it if your write-up never says the moths/bacteria/finches "tried to adapt" or "needed to change." Adaptation is the result, not the intention.

Session 3 — Where the slogan goes wrong (20–25 min)

Goal. Catch five common mistakes in ordinary talk about evolution, including the political misuse of the phrase.

Read. Lesson 3. Glossary: genetic drift (so you stop treating every change as selection).

Activity (8 minutes). Take this sentence: "In the real world the strong crush the weak; that's just survival of the fittest, so we shouldn't help people."

Mark every error. Then rewrite the scientific claim the speaker thinks they are making, and say why biology does not license the moral conclusion.

You got it if you can separate three things: (1) what natural selection does, (2) what it does not always do (cooperation can be selected; drift happens), and (3) why "it happens in nature" is not an argument that it should happen in a society.

Session 4 — The process is not a museum exhibit (20–25 min)

Goal. Point to living, current cases of selection and distinguish them from myths about human evolution.

Read. Lesson 4.

Activity (8 minutes). Choose one current domain — medicine, conservation, or agriculture. Write a six-line "selection card":

  • Trait that varies
  • How it is inherited (as far as the lesson says)
  • The environmental pressure
  • Who reproduces more
  • What a manager, doctor, or farmer might notice in a few years
  • One thing that would stop looking like selection (drift, migration, human choice, etc.)

You got it if you can name a real pressure (antibiotics, harvest, a changed habitat) and a heritable difference, and you do not claim humans have "stopped evolving" or "reached the top."

Session 5 — Test and apply (25–30 min)

Goal. Prove you can use the idea, not just recognize it.

Read. Skim the glossary once more. Then do the quiz without the answer key. After you score yourself, complete the worksheet.

Activity. Quiz first (closed notes if you can stand it). Worksheet second — the worksheet is the real test, because it asks for a new example.

You got it if you miss no more than two quiz items on meaning and mechanism, and the worksheet example has variation, heredity, and differential reproduction all present. If a step is missing, the process is not natural selection. Say so.

If you get stuck

  • The phrase is pulling you toward strength, violence, or progress: reread Lesson 1, then the first half of Lesson 3.
  • Examples feel like just-so stories: reread Lesson 2 and force every example through the three-part checklist.
  • A word will not sit still: use the glossary's "not the same as" line, not a search-engine synonym.

When the five sessions are done, you should be able to hear "survival of the fittest" in a conversation, translate it into natural selection, and tell the speaker whether they are describing a real evolutionary process or borrowing a slogan.
