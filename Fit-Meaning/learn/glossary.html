Glossary

Keep this open while you read

Short definitions as this pack uses them. Each entry has one sentence, then a "not the same as" note so the word does not slide back into ordinary English.

Fitness. Relative reproductive success in a given environment: how much a variant contributes to the next generation compared with alternatives.

Not the same as: physical strength, health in the medical sense, moral worth, or "winning" a fight.

Adaptation. A trait that spread because it raised fitness in a particular environment — or, used loosely, the process of becoming better matched to current conditions by selection.

Not the same as: an individual "getting used to" something in its own lifetime (that is acclimation or learning), or a perfect, finished design.

Natural selection. The process by which heritable differences cause differences in reproductive success, so the associated traits become more or less common across generations.

Not the same as: all of evolution (mutation, drift, and migration also change populations), and not the same as "nature is cruel."

Variation. Differences among individuals in a population in some trait.

Not the same as: differences between species, or the mere fact that change happened. Selection needs variation within the population it is filtering.

Heritability. The extent to which differences in a trait are due to genetic differences that can be passed to offspring. In this pack it is enough to ask: do offspring tend to resemble parents for this trait for genetic reasons?

Not the same as: "the trait is genetic, period." Height, for example, varies for both genetic and environmental reasons. A trait can be heritable in one population and less so in another.

Phenotype. The observable traits of an organism — color, size, behavior, biochemistry — produced by genes interacting with the environment.

Not the same as: the DNA sequence itself. Two organisms can share a genotype and still differ somewhat in phenotype if their environments differ.

Genotype. The genetic makeup of an organism at the relevant genes (or, more loosely, its full genetic recipe).

Not the same as: the visible trait. Recessive alleles, developmental noise, and environment sit between genotype and what you see.

Mutation. A change in genetic sequence, the source of new genetic variants.

Not the same as: a response to need. Mutations arise whether they would be useful or not; selection (or drift) then decides their fate. Most mutations are neutral or harmful in a given environment.

Genetic drift. Random change in how common a genetic variant is, not because it is better or worse but because of chance in who reproduces — especially powerful in small populations.

Not the same as: natural selection. If you cannot point to a systematic reproductive advantage, do not call the change adaptation.

Nearby words you will meet

Artificial selection. Humans choose who reproduces (breeding). Same logic as natural selection; the "environment" is the breeder's preference.

Population. A group of individuals of the same species that interbreed, or could — the unit whose mix of traits changes.

Differential reproduction. Unequal contribution of offspring to the next generation. The third ingredient of selection.

Social Darwinism. A political misuse of evolutionary language to justify inequality or to treat aid as interference with nature. Not a biological result. See Lesson 3.
