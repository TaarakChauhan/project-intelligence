Lesson 4

Seeing it today

Educational lesson · Fit Meaning

Natural selection is not a Victorian set piece. It is running wherever heritable variation meets differences in reproductive success. Here are places you can still point to — and human stories that get ahead of the evidence.

Medicine: resistance is selection in a dish and in a patient

Antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals, and antimalarial drugs are environmental filters. They kill or suppress some microbes and not others. If resistance is heritable — and in microbes it usually is — the resistant types become the population.

You do not need a jungle. A hospital ward will do. So will treating a viral cold with leftover antibacterial pills, which cannot cure the virus and can still select among bacteria present. MRSA — methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus — is a well-known outcome: staph strains that survive drugs that used to work.

Cancer treatment can have a similar logic inside one body. Tumors are genetically mixed. A therapy that kills most cells can leave a resistant minority, which then grows. Oncologists talk about this as selection. It is one reason combination therapies and follow-up matter.

Vaccines change the environment by making hosts harder to infect or less good at spreading some pathogens. That can favor variants that evade immunity, which is why influenza shots are updated. Details differ by disease. The skeleton does not: variation, heredity, differential success.

The practical point is not "drugs are futile." It is that every treatment is a selective pressure. The right drug, for the right infection, for the right duration — and not spraying antibiotics into livestock without need — is how you avoid running the experiment on purpose.

Conservation: you can select without meaning to

Hunt or fish the largest adults, year after year, and you are not only reducing numbers. You are favoring animals that reproduce smaller or younger, if those differences are heritable. Fisheries biologists have documented this kind of pressure in harvested populations. Trophy hunting can do the same to horns and antlers when size is the target.

Habitat change is a filter as well. A warmer stream, a fragmented forest, a night sky full of lights — each changes who reproduces. Some species shift ranges or timing. That can be plasticity (the same animal behaving differently) rather than genetic evolution. Do not call every change "adaptation" until you know whether genes shifted. The Lesson 2 checklist still applies.

Conservation that ignores selection can backfire. Breeding a tiny captive population can drain genetic variation — the raw material future environments will need. Genetic drift in a small remnant population can erase useful variants by accident. Saving a species is not only a head-count problem.

Agriculture: humans run selection on purpose

Darwin opened Origin with artificial selection: farmers and breeders choosing who reproduces. Same three-part process, with a human as the filter. Crop yield, drought tolerance, milk output — if the trait varies and is heritable, choosing parents will move the population.

Pests and weeds run the other side. A herbicide or pesticide that works well is a strong filter. Resistant weeds and insects have become common in many farming systems for the same reason resistant bacteria have: the survivors leave offspring. Crop rotation and not relying on a single chemical are attempts to weaken that filter.

This is "survival of the fittest" with the environment named: a field, a spray, a harvest date.

Humans: myths versus evidence

Myth: humans have stopped evolving because medicine and culture cancelled selection. Culture and medicine change which pressures apply. They do not abolish differences in who has children, or heritable traits. They also create new environments (cities, diets, novel pathogens). Whether selection is strong on a given human trait now is an empirical question.

Myth: civilization means the "unfit" now reproduce, so the species is degenerating. This is social Darwinism in modern clothes (Lesson 3). It assumes a single ranking of human worth, treats medical care as evolutionary sabotage, and skips the actual definition of fitness. There is no biological law that hardship produces better descendants.

Evidence, at textbook level, that human populations have evolved in the recent past: adult lactase persistence (digesting milk sugar after childhood) rose in some dairying cultures. Some high-altitude populations have genetic variants that help with low oxygen. Certain disease-resistance variants have been favored where particular infections were common. These are not claims that one population is "more evolved." They are local matches to local histories.

What the evidence does not say: that your career success is your fitness; that cruelty is selected in humans as a rule; that we are finished; or that genes are destiny for any social outcome you care about. Culture, luck, and policy sit in the environment too.

How to look at the next news story

When a headline says a pest, a microbe, a fish, or a people "evolved" something, run the filter:

  • Is there a heritable difference, or only learned behavior and one-generation change?
  • What is the actual pressure — a drug, a net, a climate shift, a mate preference?
  • Who leaves more descendants?
  • Are we watching selection, drift, migration, or someone breeding on purpose?

If you can answer those, you are no longer using "survival of the fittest" as a proverb. You are using natural selection as a tool. That was the point of the pack.
