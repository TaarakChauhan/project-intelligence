Lesson 2

How selection works

Educational lesson · Fit Meaning

Natural selection is not a mood of nature and not a plan. It is a three-part filter. If any part is missing, you do not have selection — you have some other process, or no process at all.

The three ingredients

1. Variation. Individuals in a population are not copies. They differ in size, color, metabolism, behavior, and many other traits. Selection cannot act on a trait that does not vary. If every moth is the same color, color cannot be filtered.

2. Heredity. At least some of that variation is passed from parents to offspring. The modern way to say this: differences in genotype (the genetic recipe) contribute to differences in phenotype (the observable trait), and offspring inherit genes. Darwin did not know about genes. He still needed heredity: if the survivors' offspring do not tend to resemble them, the filter resets every generation and nothing accumulates.

New heritable variation comes from mutation and from the reshuffling of existing genetic variation in sexual reproduction. Mutation is not trying to solve a problem. It is a source of raw difference, most of it useless or harmful, some of it useful in a particular environment.

3. Differential reproduction. Because of those heritable differences, some individuals leave more descendants than others. "More descendants" can mean surviving long enough to breed, attracting more mates, timing reproduction better, or raising more young. The cause is the environment as it actually is — predation, drought, disease, a chemical, a change in food — not a storybook jungle.

Do those three things for generations and the population's mix of traits shifts. Traits associated with higher reproductive success become more common. That shift is what people mean when they say a population has adapted.

Adaptation is a byproduct, not a goal

This is the sentence that saves you from cartoon evolution: nobody is aiming at adaptation.

Moths do not decide to get darker. Bacteria do not invent resistance because they "need" it. Finches do not redesign their beaks. Individuals either reproduce or they do not. The population looks more "designed" for the current environment afterward because the worse-matching variants were statistically filtered out.

That is why "in order to survive, the species evolved X" is the wrong shape of sentence. Variants that already exist, or appear by mutation, are favored or not. If the useful variant never appears, selection has nothing to promote. Many populations simply go extinct.

Adaptation is also not perfection. Selection works with the variation on hand, under constraints of anatomy, development, and history. A trait can be "good enough here" and still look clumsy. A trait can be a compromise, or leftover from an earlier environment.

Three examples, with the logic named

Peppered moths

The peppered moth (Biston betularia) comes in lighter and darker forms. That is variation, and it is heritable (a genetic difference affecting wing color).

In parts of industrial Britain, soot darkened tree surfaces and killed pale lichens. Birds eat moths they can see. On darkened surfaces the dark form was harder to spot, so dark moths were more likely to reproduce. That is differential reproduction. The dark form became more common in those areas. When air quality later improved and surfaces lightened, the advantage reversed and the light form recovered.

The moths did not choose a color. The environment changed; existing types were filtered differently. Details of early experiments have been refined; the core pattern — a heritable color difference whose success tracked a visible background — is the established example.

Antibiotic resistance

A population of bacteria is not genetically uniform. By mutation, some cells already differ in ways that reduce the effect of a drug — a pump, an enzyme that breaks it, a changed target. That is variation, and bacteria pass it on when they divide (heredity), sometimes sharing genes across cells.

Add the antibiotic. Sensitive cells die or stop dividing. Resistant cells keep reproducing. The population is soon mostly resistant. The drug did not create resistance as a wish. It created the condition in which resistance, if present, wins. Unnecessary antibiotic use is a selection experiment, not a small manners issue.

Darwin's finches

On the Galápagos, related finch populations differ in beak size and shape, matching different foods. Beak dimensions vary within a population and are heritable.

When conditions change — a drought that leaves only hard seeds, for example — birds whose beaks can handle the remaining food are more likely to survive and breed. The next generation tends to shift toward those beak measurements. When the food supply shifts, so can the advantage. Long-term field work by Peter and Rosemary Grant and colleagues made this a living demonstration: selection can be measured, and it can reverse. The birds did not plan a new beak.

A checklist you can reuse

For any claimed case of "survival of the fittest," ask:

  1. What, exactly, varied?
  2. Is that variation heritable, or could it be only diet, injury, or learning that dies with the individual?
  3. Who reproduced more, and what environmental difference caused that?
  4. Did the population's mix of types change across generations?
  5. If the environment flipped, would you predict the advantage to flip?

If you cannot answer 1–3, you do not yet have natural selection. You may have a just-so story. Lesson 3 is those stories.
